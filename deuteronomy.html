What happens in Deuteronomy

Standing on the plains of Moab, Moses looks back across forty years of wilderness and retells the story to a generation that was mostly too young to remember the plagues of Egypt or the thunder of Sinai. He restates the law not as dry repetition but as urgent instruction for the new chapter about to begin, reminding the people again and again that they were slaves, that God set them free, and that everything He asks of them flows from what He has already done for them. At the heart of the book is the Shema — the call to love the Lord with all the heart, soul, and strength — and a vision of a faith that is meant to be taught at home, talked about on the road, and bound to the very doorposts of ordinary life. Moses sets before the people a stark choice: blessing if they walk with God, consequences if they turn away, and he urges them, with the passion of a father who will not see what happens next, to choose life.

Why it matters

Deuteronomy shapes how Israel — and later, Jesus Himself — understood what it means to love God. When Jesus is tested in the wilderness, every answer He gives comes from this book. It teaches that obedience is not a transaction to earn love but a response to love already given, the grateful walk of children who know whose family they belong to.

“Hear, O Israel: The Lord our God is one Lord: And thou shalt love the Lord thy God with all thine heart, and with all thy soul, and with all thy might.” — Deuteronomy 6:4-5
