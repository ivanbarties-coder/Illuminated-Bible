What happens in Exodus

Four hundred years after Joseph's family settled in Egypt as honoured guests, their descendants have become slaves, their numbers feared by a new Pharaoh who does not remember the favour once shown. Into this suffering a baby named Moses is drawn from the river, raised in Pharaoh's own house, and later meets God in a bush that burns without being consumed. He is sent back to demand freedom for a people he barely knows, and watches ten plagues break Egypt's resistance one by one, until the final night when a lamb's blood on a doorframe becomes the difference between death and life. The sea splits, the people walk through on dry ground, and on the far side of it they become something new: no longer a family of slaves but a nation walking toward a mountain. At Sinai, God gives them His law, not as a cage but as the shape of a free people learning how to live, and He gives them something even more remarkable — instructions to build a tabernacle, a tent where He Himself will dwell among them.

Why it matters

Exodus is the Bible's primary rescue story, and every later act of redemption in Scripture borrows its language: the lamb, the blood, the passing-over of judgment, the walk from slavery into freedom. It is also the moment God moves from being the God of a family to the God who dwells among an entire people — a foreshadowing of a Father who would, in time, come to live among His children in an even closer way.

“I am the Lord thy God, which have brought thee out of the land of Egypt, out of the house of bondage.” — Exodus 20:2
